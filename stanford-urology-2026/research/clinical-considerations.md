# Clinical considerations: translating motion to meaning

This chapter explains **why** pelvic-floor kinematics might matter. It is an educational interpretation, not medical advice or a diagnosis.

## Stress urinary incontinence (SUI)
During cough, a transient rise in abdominal pressure can challenge urethral closure and pelvic support. Motion direction or magnitude alone does not establish continence status: urethral closure pressure, tissue support, timing, anatomy and measurement context also matter.

## Anticipatory contraction — “The Knack”
Voluntary contraction before and during cough is studied as a strategy to improve urethral support. The Miller et al. reading in `02-phase-2-imaging-analysis/reading/` provides background; it is **external literature**, not an intervention outcome measured in this project.

## Valsalva versus cough
Valsalva is a sustained load; cough is a rapid transient. Their kinematic profiles should not be compared without aligned definitions of event onset, pressure, and timebase.

## From ultrasound to candidate biomarkers
Potential research variables include displacement magnitude, cranial/caudal component, time-to-peak, peak speed, peak acceleration, and between-maneuver differences. These are **candidate features**, not validated biomarkers or established diagnostic thresholds.

## Clinical validation pathway
Future work would need independently verified annotations, calibration, test-retest reliability, clinician-reviewed interpretations, appropriate control/comparison cohorts, and links to relevant clinical outcomes before any diagnostic claim.

## Limitations
The source presentation reports 23 asymptomatic subjects; it does not establish a validated SUI classifier, clinical sensitivity/specificity, prospective outcomes, or treatment effectiveness. Website traces are explicitly synthetic and must not be confused with participant measurements.
